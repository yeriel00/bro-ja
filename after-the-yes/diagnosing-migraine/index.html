After the Yes · Step 1

How to evaluate a patient for migraine

For many patients, a focused history and neurological examination can support a clinical migraine diagnosis. Screen for red flags and consider other causes before confirming the diagnosis.1

  2. Step 2Treating migraine
  3. Step 3Ongoing care

Start with three migraine screening questions

Two or three yes answers on the ID Migraine screen signal a 93% likelihood of migraine. The screen doesn’t rule out secondary headache.

  1. Does light bother you when you have a headache?
  2. Have headaches limited your activities for a day or more in the last three months?
  3. Are you nauseated or sick to your stomach when you have a headache?

  • Migraine without aura

    Symptoms last 4 to 72 hours, often affect one side of the head, and have a pulsing quality. Routine activity makes them worse, accompanied by nausea or light and sound sensitivity.2

  • Migraine with aura

    Reversible neurological symptoms, most often visual, that build over 5 to 20 minutes and resolve within an hour. Many patients with aura also have migraine without it.3

Review prescription and OTC medication use

Ask about OTC and combination products by name and how often your patient takes them. Many patients don’t count these as medication, and frequent use can lead to medication overuse headache.4

Identify red flags

While most patients won’t have any, red flags fall into two groups, and each calls for a different response.5

  • Needs urgent evaluation

    • Patient experiencing fever
    • New neurological deficit
    • Thunderclap onset reaching peak intensity within a minute
    • Onset during sexual activity

  • Consider imaging or more tests

    • Patient experiencing new or worsening headache
    • Significant change in pattern
    • New onset after 50
    • Signs of systemic illness, predisposing conditions including pregnancy
    • Headache that worsens when standing, starts with coughing or exertion, or wakes the patient from sleep
    • Pain always on the same side
    • No response to treatment

What isn’t a red flag

Mid-face pain that patients interpret as sinus trouble and pain at the base of the skull are common migraine symptoms. A stable pattern with a normal neurological exam rarely needs imaging.6

Explain the diagnosis and next steps

Name migraine as a neurological disease with effective treatment, and take your patient’s experience seriously. If they’ve heard sinus or stress before, update that explanation without discrediting earlier care.

What changes for specific patients

  • Hormones, contraception, and pregnancy

    Note whether symptoms follow the menstrual cycle and ask about aura, which shapes contraception choices. We recommend yearly migraine screening from adolescence through menopause.7

    • Routine Migraine Screening in Women’s HealthGuideline

  • Children and teens

    Migraine in children often runs shorter and affects both sides of the head. A focused history and exam help you tell primary from secondary headache.8

    • Diagnosing and Treating Pediatric MigraineQuick Guide

  • Older adults

    New headache after 50 calls for evaluation of other causes, including giant cell arteritis, before you confirm migraine.9
