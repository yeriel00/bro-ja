After the Yes · Step 2

Treating migraine

Every patient with migraine needs an acute plan, and some also need prevention. Treatment options have expanded in recent years, and you can start most of them in frontline care.1

  3. Step 3Ongoing care

Set expectations together

Talk through what treatment can realistically do and how long it takes to work. Ask your patient to track migraine days and what they disrupt, and discuss common triggers without placing blame.

Patient Guides to Share

Match acute treatment to severity

Aim for relief within two hours, a return to daily activities, and little need for rescue medication.2

  • Mild symptoms

    If your patient can keep up with their routine, start with common analgesics and NSAIDs.

  • Moderate to severe symptoms

    If symptoms disrupt the routine, use triptans, gepants, ditans, or DHE, alone or with an NSAID or antiemetic. Choose a non-oral route for vomiting or fast-peaking symptoms.

Treat early, within limits

Encourage early use and set limits: 14 days a month for simple analgesics and nine for other acute medications to prevent medication overuse headache.3

When to add prevention

Offer prevention based on how often symptoms occur and how much they disrupt daily life. We consider CGRP-targeting therapies a first-line option.4

Choosing a preventive treatment

Pick an option that also treats a comorbidity when possible, and weigh the side effects that matter most to your patient, such as weight change or sleepiness.

Give it time

Prevention succeeds when migraine days drop by half or more after a three- to six-month trial at the target dose.5

Getting treatment covered

Prior authorization, step therapy, and quantity limits can delay care. Documenting migraine days and past treatments makes approval easier.6

What changes for specific patients

  • Hormones, contraception, and pregnancy

    Weigh stroke risk before prescribing estrogen-containing contraception to patients with aura, and consider options for menstrual migraine. Know which treatments to use or avoid in pregnancy and breastfeeding, and when to refer.7

    • Contraceptive Options for Women With MigraineQuick Guide

  • Children and teens

    Choose first-line acute options for children, learn which migraine-specific treatments are approved for adolescents, and lead prevention with behavioral approaches.8

    • Pediatric Migraine Treatment Options and Further EvaluationQuick Guide

  • Older adults

    Consider cardiovascular risk and current medications when you choose acute and preventive treatment.9
