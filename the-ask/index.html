The Ask

One question to help identify patients with migraine

Developed with input from frontline clinicians, this question is designed to fit into routine visits and uncover headaches that interfere with daily life.1

“In the past month, has a headache interfered with your work, school, family, or other activities?”

Why this question?

Many people with migraine do not receive a diagnosis and recommended treatment. Asking every patient about headache-related interference can reveal symptoms they might not otherwise mention.2

  • Quick to ask

    A single question can fit into existing intake forms, rooming, or clinical visits. It takes just seconds to ask and can be documented alongside other routine screening questions, making migraine screening easier to incorporate into everyday care.

  • Focus on function

    Patients may normalize symptoms or manage them quietly with OTC medication. Asking about daily life reveals what’s at stake.3

  • Ask what interfered, not what was missed

    Patients who show up to work or school through symptoms would answer no to “missed” but yes to “interfered.”3

  • Make headache part of routine care

    Asking about headaches helps patients recognize migraine as a legitimate health concern, especially when they’ve spent years dismissing or normalizing their symptoms. That recognition can extend beyond the exam room. Sharing what they’ve learned may help friends and family recognize their own symptoms and seek care. One question can have an impact well beyond the patient being asked.

See the Case for Asking

Make it part of your existing workflow

Asking every patient matters more than who asks or when. The Ask is most effective when it’s built into the existing workflow, tied to a clear next step, and tested with your team before rollout.

  • On the intake form

    Patients answer on a paper or digital intake form before the visit, so a “yes” arrives with the rest of their history.

  • During rooming

    A nurse or medical assistant asks alongside vitals and other routine questions, then flags a “yes” for the clinician.

  • In the exam room

    The clinician asks while reviewing symptoms or the reason for the visit and can follow up on a “yes” during the same visit or at a dedicated follow-up.

  • As an EHR prompt

    A prompt or template in the EHR reminds staff or the clinician to ask, helping make screening a consistent part of routine care.

When the answer is yes

A “yes” does not establish a migraine diagnosis. It identifies a patient who may benefit from further questions, assessment for red flags, and a plan for diagnosis and treatment.4

After the Yes: Guidance and Resources

Even with two minutes left

When a “yes” appears late in the visit, identifying the likely diagnosis and scheduling a follow-up still moves care forward.

Share it with your team

“In the past month, has a headache interfered with your work, school, family, or other activities?”

Share the question with staff who manage intake, rooming, and documentation so patients receive a consistent screening experience.
