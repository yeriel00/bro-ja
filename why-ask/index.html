Why Ask

The case for asking

Migraine affects daily life for millions of people, but many never receive a diagnosis or treatment. A routine question about headache-related interference gives clinicians a practical way to identify patients who may need further evaluation.

A common disease with a significant impact

Migraine is a neurological disease that can involve sensory, vestibular, cognitive, and gastrointestinal symptoms, sometimes without severe headache. It affects people across demographics and often disrupts work, school, and family life.1

  • 1 in 6

    U.S. adults live with migraine or severe headache.2

  • 2×

    About twice as common in women.3

  • 113M

    workdays lost to migraine each year in the U.S.4

  • 42%

    live with moderate or greater migraine-related disability.5

The diagnosis gap

Patients with migraine may see a clinician for other concerns without ever discussing their headaches. When symptoms go unmentioned, opportunities for diagnosis and effective treatment are missed.6

  1. Currently consulting a clinician738.7%
  2. Consulting and accurately diagnosed727.0%
  3. Consulting, diagnosed, and on appropriate treatment714.4%

Wider for some patients

Diagnosis rates fall further for Black and Hispanic patients compared with white patients, and for men compared with women.8

What frontline clinicians told us

AHS surveyed and interviewed clinicians across primary care, women’s health, urgent care, and specialty settings to understand what gets in the way of migraine diagnosis and which tools would help in everyday practice.9

  • 133

    clinicians screened.9

  • 17

    in-depth interviews.9

  • 8

    specialties represented.9

Clinicians want practical support

Clinicians reported confidence in managing migraine, especially with more experience diagnosing it. They also asked for concise tools, actionable guidance, and a clear path after screening.9

Patients often do not bring up headaches

Patients may attribute recurring symptoms to stress, sinus problems, or hormones, or dismiss them as “just a headache.” A routine question helps identify symptoms they might otherwise leave out.9
